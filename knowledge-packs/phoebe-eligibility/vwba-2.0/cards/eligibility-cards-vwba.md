## Design decision — a guide, not a gate

**Maintainer's ruling, 23 Sep 2026.** It replaces the ruling of 20 Aug 2026, whose wording is in
this pack's CHANGELOG under 0.4.5. All six criteria still have to be met for a project to generate
a benefit; what changed is what a miss means. A criterion not met today is one of three things:
fixable, with a cited route from the guidebook or a real published project; unknown, with what to
find out; or truly blocked, and only the cards say which criteria can ever be that. The answer for
the pathway is a readiness read, likely eligible, likely not, or not enough known yet, never pass
or fail. There is still no weighting, no partial credit and no averaging across criteria; a
readiness read is a sort, not a score. The rule itself has one home, *Pace and posture* in
AGENT_RULES.md; the worksheet carries the three kinds of miss and the read once item A18 builds.
**Until then the worksheet has its three states, and a criterion not met is "Not yet" with a route
forward**, which was always the posture.

**Every "not yet" has to arrive with a route forward.** A verdict that only reports failure is not
an acceptable output. Whenever Phoebe finds a criterion unmet, she states what would change that:
the specific evidence, document, consultation, or design change that would move the project from
*not yet* to *met*. The posture is the rule.

**Figure 3 is not only absolute.** It loops a project that misses back to the implementer to
revisit scope and re-evaluate, and only the second miss ends in "not eligible" (Step 2.3,
Figure 3, p. 19). The build follows the whole figure, loop included.

## How these six fit together

All six have to be satisfied. Figure 3 on p. 19 routes a project that falls short of any one of
them out of eligibility, with an intermediate option: go back to the implementer, see whether the
scope can be reworked, and assess it again. Only a project clearing all six is eligible to
generate a volumetric water benefit.

These criteria govern **whether a project can generate a benefit at all** — not what a sponsor is
then allowed to say publicly about it. Appendix A sets the claim-making question aside deliberately
(p. 32). The pointer to where that question *is* handled — Step 6 — comes from the summary box on
p. 20, not from Appendix A itself.

Appendix A also frames what the six are collectively meant to screen for (p. 32): benefits resting
on sound and consistent calculation, grounded in a real local context and going beyond what would
have happened anyway, not helping one party at another's expense, not producing unintended harm to
those depending on the water, and still checkable in later years for the life of the claims.

## Card 1 — A working route to a countable benefit

**The rule in plain words.** The project has to change the water picture for the better — either by
altering how water moves through the catchment, or by easing a shared water problem, or both. That
improvement then has to be countable as a volume. The count comes from comparing the situation with
the project against the situation without it, worked out either with a VWBA method or with another
method resting on the same principles.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 1 — "Established pathway for
a quantifiable VWB", p. 32. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What a project owner would be asked to show.**

- The objective of the activity, stated.
- Confirmation the activity works on a shared water challenge.
- The indicator chosen to measure the benefit.
- The calculation method named — and where it is not a VWBA method, the reasoning for why it holds
  to the same principles.

---

## Card 2 — The problem is real in that specific place

**The rule in plain words.** Whatever water problem the project takes on has to be one that actually
exists in that catchment or area. It should also be documented or well-understood locally — at
community, basin, or regional level — and it has to matter to the people and bodies who depend on
that water, including communities, agencies and tribes.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 2 — "Water challenges
addressed relevant to the catchment or area of interest", p. 32. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What a project owner would be asked to show.**

- The project site, mapped.
- How the local water challenges were identified — desktop research, or engagement with the
  community and others relying on or advocating for the water.
- An explicit link from the project's objective and activity to one of those identified challenges.

---

## Card 3 — Support from inside and from the neighbours

**The rule in plain words.** Two audiences have to be behind the project. Inside the sponsoring
organisation there needs to be genuine buy-in. Outside it, the groups connected to that water —
communities, agencies, Indigenous peoples, and others who rely on it or speak for it — need to be
broadly supportive of the hydrologic benefit being proposed. How deep the consultation goes depends
on local conditions, and can be run by implementing partners or others with local knowledge. A
high-risk project, or one in a place where reputation is delicate, deserves more of it.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 3 — "Internal buy-in and
general support from external water resources entities", p. 33. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What a project owner would be asked to show.**

**This list is depth of proof. It is not a second set of gates.** There is one criterion on this
card — both audiences are behind the project — and the four items below are how deeply that single
thing is evidenced, not four further tests a project has to pass one by one. **How much is asked
for scales with local conditions**, exactly as the rule above says: a high-risk project, or one in a
place where reputation is delicate, is asked for more of it. A project carrying less of this has
thinner proof of the same one thing; it has not failed four separate checks.

**Phoebe must never present these four as four gates**, must never count them, and must never say a
project met three of four. The criterion is met or it is not yet met, and where it is not yet met
the route forward names the evidence that would carry it — which is what these four describe.

- A community consultation, or desktop evidence gathered before the project began, establishing
  that it matters to others.
- Any concerns surfaced, along with thinking about what they imply — who gains, and which values
  the project serves.
- Evidence of working with implementers to hold trade-offs down.
- The reasoning behind the decision to back the project, communicated to interested parties.

## Card 4 — The benefit is genuinely additional

**The rule in plain words.** The project has to produce a gain, or head off a harm, that would not
have occurred otherwise. Work a sponsor is legally obliged to carry out for compliance does not
qualify. Two situations sit outside that bar. First, where a sponsor is legally required to fund
environmental work through a wider corporate social responsibility obligation, that money may go to
a project generating a benefit, provided the additionality and the intent are both present. Second,
where the legal duty sits with the site owner but that owner has neither the capacity nor the
engagement to deliver a real improvement, a sponsor who steps in may count the resulting benefit.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 4 — "Change delivered beyond
the without-project conditions (change that would not have happened without the activity)", p. 33.
Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What a project owner would be asked to show.**

- Confirmation that the sponsor is not under a legal requirement to do this work.
- Where the site owner *is* under such a requirement, written reasons why compliance would not
  happen without the sponsor's support — or why the activity reaches past what the law demands.

---

## Card 5 — A route to keep checking the volumes

**The rule in plain words.** The design has to include, from the outset, a way of keeping track of
the project and reporting on it once the work is done. That means arrangements that persist —
enough to keep confirming the project still does what it was built to do, for as long as the
benefit is being claimed, or across the project's whole lifetime if that is what the sponsor wants.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 5 — "Established pathway to
track project volumetric outputs", p. 33. Short restatement at p. 20. Appendix E carries the
detailed guidance on building such a plan, beginning at p. 62.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What a project owner would be asked to show.**

- A tracking and reporting plan, drawn up with the implementer at selection or contracting time —
  not after the fact.
- The outputs and outcomes it will track, clearly identified.
- Evidence the capacity and resources exist to run it, in money and in people.

---

## Card 6 — The downsides have been looked for and cut back

**The rule in plain words.** Someone has to have gone looking for the ways this project could hurt
somebody or backfire, understood what they found, and acted to reduce it. That review can happen
before implementation, after it, or both. The guidance offers examples of the kind of thing to look
for: farmers harvesting less after changing practice, vegetation thinning once invasive species are
pulled out, a stream's base flow dropping where reclaimed wastewater is used, and water access
projects that serve one party at another's cost.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 6 — "Trade-offs assessed,
understood, and minimized", p. 33. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What a project owner would be asked to show.**

- A desktop review, consultation with others, and/or technical evidence — gathered before the
  project started — identifying trade-offs and what they imply. How deep this goes scales with
  local conditions and the size of the project.
- Closer attention where the risk of adverse outcomes is high, or the region is reputationally
  sensitive.
- For large-scale work with complicated trade-offs: evidence they were assessed *and* communicated
  to the relevant parties before the activity began.
- Evidence of working with implementers to minimise what was found.
- The justification for supporting the project, communicated to interested parties.
- Note that the guidance allows extra latitude for transformational, very large-scale projects
  where mapping every trade-off is impractical or not feasible.

**The latitude does not open the gate.** Figure 3 (p. 19) is absolute, and the maintainer's ruling
of 20 Aug 2026 is that the build follows Figure 3 — so **criterion 6 is a hard gate for a
transformational project exactly as it is for any other.** What the latitude changes is **how
exhaustively trade-offs must be mapped** where mapping every one of them is impractical. It does not
change whether they were looked for, understood, and cut back. A project that skipped that work has
not met criterion 6, however large or transformational it is.

**Phoebe must never offer the latitude as an exemption**, and must never let project scale stand in
for the evidence. Where a very large project cannot map every trade-off, the honest answer says what
*was* assessed and what was left — it does not report the criterion met on the strength of the
project's size.
