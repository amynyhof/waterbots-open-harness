**For Phoebe (beta) — Eligibility and Feasibility specialist. Pack `vwba-2.0`.**

**Status: approved by the maintainer, 23 Sep 2026.** Graded at the repository root as a draft

Nine cards, one cited fix per common gap on the water pathway. **A route is a rewrite, in two or
three short sentences, of something the guidebook or one real published project says can be done.**
Never a fix the engineer thought of. Where no such source exists for a gap, there is no card, and
Phoebe says so and offers a person instead. All wording is a rewrite; no sentence is copied. The
source PDFs stay in `sources-local/`, gitignored, never published.

---

## How a route is used, and what it is not

**A route hangs on a Fixable row.** When Phoebe finds a criterion not met and the card's "Can it be
fixed?" line says *yes* or *depends*, she names the route by its id, and the console renders the
route's words with its citation under the row. She never writes a route in her own words that has
no card. A row with no route card behind it cannot be Fixable; it is Unknown, or the offer of a
person.

**A route is a way to try, not a promise.** Every card says so in one line. Taking a route does
not make the criterion Met; the evidence the card asks for does.

**Reading level.** Two or three short sentences a twelve-year-old could read, per the rulebook.
The card gate prints a grade figure against a grade-6 target and does not enforce it (her rider on
R8).

## Route R-1 — Rework the scope with the implementer

**The gap.** Any criterion not met the first time, on card 1 to card 6.

**The route in plain words.** A project that misses a criterion is not finished. Go back to the
people who would build it, talk about what could change in the plan, and check the criteria again.
The guidebook draws this loop itself; only a second miss ends in "not eligible".

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Step 2.3, Figure 3, "Flow diagram outlining
project selection process", p. 19.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** A reworked scope is checked again from the start; it is not met
because it was reworked.

## Route R-2 — Name the shared water challenge from the catchment context

**The gap.** Card 2: the water problem is not yet shown to be real in that place.

**The route in plain words.** Start with the catchment, not the project. Learn its boundaries, its
surface water and groundwater, who governs it, and what water problems people there already know
about. Desktop research works; talking to the people who depend on the water, especially those hurt
most by its problems, works better and may show efforts already under way to join.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Step 1, "Understand the local catchment
context", pp. 16–17; Appendix A, criterion 2, "What a project owner would be asked to show", p. 32.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** Finding a challenge does not make it the project's; the project's
objective still has to be tied to it, which is what card 2 asks for.

## Route R-3 — Pick the indicator and the method from the activity table

**The gap.** Card 1: no route to a countable benefit is named yet.

**The route in plain words.** Name the one thing the project is meant to do for the water, then
look the activity up in the guidebook's table. The table says what to measure and which method
counts it for the common activities. If it is hard to choose, the guidebook says to bring in a
subject matter expert.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Step 3, "Quantify the VWBs of project
activities", and Step 3.1, "Identify the primary volumetric objective", p. 21; Appendix C, Table
C-1, pp. 37–39.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** An activity outside the table is not ruled out, but the table does
not count it; a method resting on the same principles has to be named and argued.

## Route R-4 — Run a consultation before deciding

**The gap.** Card 3: support from inside the sponsor or from the people around the water is not
yet shown.

**The route in plain words.** Before the project is chosen, ask the people who depend on that
water what they think of it, or gather the desktop evidence that it matters to them. Write down any
worries that come up, who gains, and what was done with the implementer to keep the trade-offs
small. Then tell the people involved why the project was backed.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 3, "Internal buy-in and
general support from external water resources entities", and its evidence list, p. 33.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** How deep the consultation has to go depends on the place; a risky or
sensitive place is asked for more, and one round may not be enough.

## Route R-5 — Show the work goes beyond a legal duty

**The gap.** Card 4, where the "Can it be fixed?" line says *depends*: some legal duty touches the
work.

**The route in plain words.** Two doors stay open when a law is involved. If the sponsor must spend
money on environmental work under a wider corporate social responsibility duty, that money may
still fund a benefit, as long as the project is additional and meant to be. If the legal duty
belongs to the site owner, and that owner cannot or will not do the work, a sponsor who steps in
may count the benefit; write down why compliance would not have happened otherwise.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 4, "Change delivered
beyond the without-project conditions", the two situations outside the bar, and its evidence list,
p. 33.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** Work the sponsor itself must do by law stays out, whatever the
paperwork says.

## Route R-6 — Make a tracking and reporting plan

**The gap.** Card 5: there is no plan yet to keep checking the volumes.

**The route in plain words.** Make the plan before the project starts, with the people who will
run it. Decide what will be checked each year: that the work was done, and either the water
measured or the things the number depends on, such as permits, rainfall, structures and upkeep.
Decide how often and for how long, and put the cost and the people for it in the budget.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix E, "Making a tracking and reporting
plan", pp. 62–64, and Table E-1, p. 64; Step 4.3, "Make a tracking and reporting plan", p. 25.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** A plan on paper is not capacity; card 5 also asks for evidence the
money and the people exist to run it.

---

## Route R-7 — Look for the downsides before starting

**The gap.** Card 6: nobody has yet looked for the ways the project could hurt someone or backfire.

**The route in plain words.** Before the work begins, look for who might lose: a desktop review,
talking to the people affected, or technical evidence, as much as the size and the risk of the place
call for. Understand what was found, work with the implementer to make it smaller, and tell the
people involved why the project is still worth backing.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix A, criterion 6, "Trade-offs assessed,
understood, and minimized", and its evidence list, p. 33.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** A very large project gets latitude on how many trade-offs it can
map, never on whether it looked.

## Route R-8 — Count a drinking-water project as Volume Provided, as a published project did

**The gap.** Card 1 and applies card W2, for a project that gives households or a community access
to drinking water and does not know how it would be counted.

**The route in plain words.** The guidebook has a method for this: Volume Provided counts the water
a new, restored or improved supply gives people each year, from meters if there are any, else from
what the system can deliver or from how many people get access, how much each uses, and how many
days a year. One real project has been counted this way and published: households on the Navajo
Nation given permanent in-home water from tanks and delivery, reported as volume provided.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix D, "D-3. Volume Provided method",
pp. 44–45, "Access to household or community water supply". LimnoTech for Meta, *Volumetric Water
Benefits: 2023 Report*, 25 June 2024, "Navajo Community Water Supply", pp. 25–26; Appendix 1,
p. 39.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for the
guidebook. For the published project: <https://sustainability.atmeta.com/asset/2023-volumetric-water-benefits/>,
Meta's own address for the report, confirmed by the maintainer on 23 Sep 2026. Neither is a mirror
or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** The water has to meet local quality standards and the people have to
have reasonable access; a supply that fails either is not counted by this method.

## Route R-9 — Put the agreement in writing

**The gap.** Card 5, and card 3 where support is real but not recorded.

**The route in plain words.** Once the project is chosen, write an agreement with the people who
will build and run it: a contract, a memorandum, a funding agreement. Put in it who does what,
the timeline, how and when progress is reported, and how the money flows, including the money for
reporting and upkeep over the years the benefit is claimed.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Step 4.4, "Formalize contribution, commitment,
and support for water stewardship project", p. 25.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**What it does not promise.** An agreement records support and a plan; it does not create the
support card 3 asks about.
