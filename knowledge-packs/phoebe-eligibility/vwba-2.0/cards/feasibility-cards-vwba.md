# Feasibility cards — VWBA 2.0, Appendix B

**For Phoebe (beta) — Eligibility and Feasibility specialist.**

**Status: approved by the maintainer, 21 Aug 2026.**

Ten cards, one per selection consideration in Appendix B, none skipped. All wording is a rewrite;
no sentence is copied. The source PDF stays in `sources-local/`, which is gitignored and never
publishes — this file cites and links it.

---

## These are not requirements. Read this before any card.

**Every consideration in this file applies only to projects that have already cleared the
eligibility gate.** They help choose well among projects that are already eligible. **None of them
is a bar to entry, and failing one is not a failure at all.**

The manual is unambiguous about this. Appendix B opens by placing these considerations on top of
Appendix A's essential criteria, and says a project needs none of them in order to produce a
volumetric water benefit (p. 34). The Step 2.3 summary box says the same from the other direction:
they help ensure value and success, but a project does not need them to generate benefits (p. 20).

So the two sets are different in kind, and Phoebe must never blur them:

| | Eligibility — Appendix A | Feasibility — Appendix B |
|---|---|---|
| **What it does** | Decides whether a project can generate a benefit at all | Helps choose among projects that already can |
| **How it behaves** | Hard gate. Miss one criterion and the project is not eligible | Guidance. Weigh them; none of them excludes |
| **Number** | Six criteria | Ten considerations |
| **A shortfall means** | Not eligible, with a route forward | Nothing disqualifying — a factor to weigh |

**Two further points the manual makes, both of which constrain how Phoebe may speak here.**

- **They carry no ranking.** Appendix B states plainly that the considerations are not in priority
  order, because companies weigh them differently (p. 34). The numbering below is the manual's
  sequence and nothing more. **Phoebe must not present a lower number as more important.**
- **Weighing them is the company's job, not Phoebe's.** The manual leaves the relative importance
  to each company. Phoebe can lay out what a consideration covers and what the manual says to look
  at; deciding how much it counts for is the user's call.

**How a shortfall should be voiced.** Never *this project fails consideration 4.* Instead: *the
implementation timeline is not yet pinned down — here is what the manual suggests looking at, and
here is why it may matter to you.* Guidance, offered; never a verdict, delivered.

## How the ten are grouped

The grouping is the manual's own, stated in the Appendix B introduction on p. 34:

- **Considerations 1–5 — attributes contributing to the likelihood of success.** Will this project
  actually work, and keep working?
- **Considerations 6–10 — attributes providing added impact or value.** Beyond working, what else
  does this project bring?

Each consideration in the source carries three parts, and every card below carries all three: what
the consideration covers, why it matters, and how a practitioner evaluates an opportunity against
it.

---

# Group one — likelihood of success

**Considerations 1 to 5.** Whether the project will work, and go on working.

## Card B-1 — Low risk of the project failing or underdelivering

**The consideration in plain words.** Ask whether the design is sturdy enough to keep producing a
benefit as years pass. Look for the ways it could fail or fall short of expectations, and check
that something is actually in place to handle the serious ones — including the maintenance and
repair that can be seen coming.

**Why it matters.** A project less likely to fail, and one with maintenance and repair arranged,
stands a better chance of delivering the outputs and impacts hoped for, and lets a practitioner
claim benefits with more confidence as time passes. There is a second reason that is easy to miss:
**if a project does fail and someone else's money is needed to repair it, how the benefit is
divided among funders may have to be reopened — and the original claims can shrink.**

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 1 — "Minimal risk of
project failure or underperformance", p. 34. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Talk to the people implementing the project to understand the design and the assumptions sitting
  underneath it, and what they plan to do to keep it going over time.
- A project without this settled is not disqualified. It is a project where the durability of any
  claim is less certain, and that is information for the sponsor, not a verdict on the project.

---

## Card B-2 — The implementer is ready and able

**The consideration in plain words.** Two questions about the organisation doing the work. Is it
*ready* — does it have a route for contracting and receiving money, and are the permits, approvals
and planning steps both moving and achievable? And is it *able* — does it have the staff,
knowledge, authorisation, skills, and track record on comparable work? The manual adds one more
signal: a track record of working well alongside other practitioners who have backed this kind of
project before.

**Why it matters.** An implementer that is not ready, or cannot get the permits and approvals,
runs into obstacles that stop a project or hold it up.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 2 — "Project
implementer readiness and capacity", p. 34. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- The manual's own instruction here is brief: work through the traits and conditions above and
  confirm them.
- Phoebe should not invent extra tests. The list is the list.

---

## Card B-3 — The money is clear, and so is who claims what

**The consideration in plain words.** Establish what the project costs in total, and what the
separate pieces cost — the manual names discovery, design, construction, long-term maintenance and
tracking — along with what each company is putting in, so it is clear whether the financing is
actually secured. Weigh the risk of expected money not arriving, and work out who all the parties
are. Then settle an approach for dividing the benefit among the funders. Ask the implementer
whether costs are likely to move later.

**Why it matters.** Where costs and the division of benefit are murky, three things follow:
unexpected funding gaps, delays, or **the same volume being counted twice without anyone
intending it.** Getting this clear also shows whether the project could scale if more money
appeared, and reveals funding dependencies between phases that could affect benefit delivery.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 3 — "Clarity on project
costs and cost shares among funders", p. 34. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Get cost information and any funding gaps from the implementer.
- Ask who the other sponsors are, and work with them on a division of benefit that can be defended.
- The double-counting point connects to *attribution* in the definitions set — worth reaching for
  when a project owner asks how shared funding affects what they can say.

---

## Card B-4 — The timeline is known and realistic

**The consideration in plain words.** Work with the implementer so the schedule and its key
milestones are both understood and genuinely achievable. This matters most where a company means to
use the resulting benefits against goals that have deadlines attached — and the manual notes such
goals can involve progress reported along the way as well as at the end.

**Why it matters.** When the schedule and the milestone dates are unclear, delays nobody planned
for become more likely.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 4 — "Feasible project
implementation timeline", p. 34, continuing onto p. 35, where the evaluation guidance finishes.
Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Get the schedule and milestone detail from the implementer, and form a view of what could cause
  delay.
- Keep talking to them regularly, so progress against milestones stays visible rather than being
  discovered late.

---

## Card B-5 — How long the benefits last, and whether that matches your timeline

**The consideration in plain words.** How long a project keeps delivering varies, and what it
varies with is the kind of activity and the way it is funded. The manual contrasts two ends:
nature-based solutions and infrastructure work usually deliver over a long horizon, while paying
for environmental services or changing farming practice may deliver over a much shorter one — it
gives one to three years as the example. **Neither end is the better answer.** The question is
whether the duration a project actually offers lines up with the timeline the sponsor is working
to.

**Why it matters.** A project still delivering ten years out or more may suit a company with
deadlines ahead of it, or one wanting to affect a shared water challenge over a long stretch — but
a shorter-lived project is not thereby worse, only differently matched. Being clear about duration
up front is described as critical for one specific reason: **it is how the investing company and
the implementer avoid misunderstanding each other.** A mismatch discovered late is the failure
mode this consideration exists to prevent.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 5 — "Anticipated
duration of VWBs consistent with desired timeline", p. 35. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Ask the implementer how long delivery should last for that activity and that funding structure.
- Check there is a way to report as often as wanted, right through the period benefits are claimed
  for.
- **Allow for the future being uncertain.** The manual asks explicitly that delivery be considered
  in light of a changing climate and ecosystems that do not stay still — a rare instance of it
  telling a practitioner to hold a projection loosely.

# Group two — added impact or value

**Considerations 6 to 10.** Beyond working, what else the project brings.

## Card B-6 — The location fits the goals being pursued

**The consideration in plain words.** Check that where the project sits actually matches the water
goals stated. The manual gives two shapes this can take. A goal may call for a hydrologic link,
direct or indirect, to the water a site draws on — or simply for nearness to the site or to the
local community affected. Or a goal may instead call for the project to connect to the company's
value chain, meaning its supply chain or its customers.

**Why it matters.** Water is a local matter, and goals ought to reflect local conditions. A project
whose location fits the stated goals is what makes a claim against those goals defensible.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 6 — "Location relevant
to water goals", p. 35. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- A desktop review of what the project looks like, set against the company's water stewardship
  goals.
- Note this consideration is about the goals a company has set itself. **It is not a second version
  of eligibility criterion 2**, which asks whether a real shared water challenge exists in the
  catchment. A project can sit squarely on a genuine challenge and still be a poor fit for one
  company's particular goals, and that is a fit question, not a soundness one.

---

## Card B-7 — The project does more than move water

**The consideration in plain words.** Ask whether the project can produce gains beyond volumes of
water, and whether it can serve other company goals — the manual lists water quality, water access,
carbon, biodiversity, and social or economic impacts. It attaches a practical caveat: reporting
those extra gains may mean tracking more than the water alone.

**Why it matters.** A project delivering more than volume addresses a shared water challenge more
completely, and may matter more to the people and bodies who depend on that water. Some companies
have set goals reaching past volumetric benefits, and these projects can serve them.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 7 — "Opportunity to
deliver multiple benefits", p. 35. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Raise it with the implementer at the start, not once the project is running.
- The implementer should record the without-project condition, and may have to widen monitoring to
  capture the additional gains.
- Describe those gains in words. Where a method exists to put a number on them, use it.
- **Note the asymmetry.** The volumetric benefit has a prescribed method behind it; these extra
  benefits may only be describable qualitatively. Phoebe should not let a qualitative account be
  mistaken for a quantified one.

---

## Card B-8 — The project opens the door to something larger

**The consideration in plain words.** Some projects matter mostly for what they make possible. They
can set off action with far more impact than their own, and serve as stepping stones toward
large-scale, transformational work on shared water challenges. They can also create openings to
shape water governance for the better. The manual points to early-phase work in particular —
planning, design, permitting, pilots — the sort that lays groundwork for bigger things to follow.

**Why it matters.** There is both a need and an opening for companies to back large-scale
transformational work, and much of it needs early money to clear obstacles and open the way to
scaling up. The manual adds a second case: **enabling projects can matter most where little other
water stewardship work is happening.**

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 8 — "Enabling
projects", p. 35. Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Ask the implementer whether there is scope here for enabling, repeatable, or scalable work.
- **A caution Phoebe should carry.** This consideration values a project for what it unlocks
  later. That is not the same as a countable benefit now — an enabling project must still meet all
  six eligibility criteria on its own account, including criterion 1's requirement of a countable
  route. Promise of what may follow does not substitute for that.

---

## Card B-9 — The approach itself breaks new ground

**The consideration in plain words.** Projects that produce benefits *and* try something new — in
how they are financed, in the technology used, or in building market systems that can scale — may
be ranked higher. On money, the manual points to financing that is sustainable, leveraged, or
capable of unlocking further funds, which can open fresh routes to generating benefits, help them
scale, or raise their impact. On technology, piloting something new can lead to solutions spreading
through the market, or produce an investment structure that suits a wider range of sponsors.

**Why it matters.** There is a need to speed up and unlock chances for companies to set off
large-scale transformational work with significant impact on shared water challenges.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 9 — "Innovative
strategies", p. 35, continuing onto p. 36, where the reasoning and the evaluation guidance sit.
Short restatement at p. 20.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Look at opportunities involving new finance and investment arrangements. The manual's examples
  are investment funds, microloans, revolving funds, arrangements that channel repayments back into
  maintaining the project, and projects that improve the policy landscape.
- Look also at new technologies and at market systems.
- **This consideration and card B-10 are the two written in the language of priority** — the manual
  says such projects *may be considered with higher priority* here, and *may be considered to be
  higher priority* at B-10. In both cases that is a ranking aid among projects already eligible,
  never a threshold. No other consideration uses priority wording.

---

## Card B-10 — Others are working on it too

**The consideration in plain words.** Projects that produce benefits *and* create room for working
together — through pooled funding and collective action, which the manual glosses as co-designing
and co-funding — may be ranked higher. Where several corporate funders work alongside on-the-ground
implementers, the manual says the value shows up as greater impact, more transparency, and a better
story to tell. Collective action can also let one company contribute across a wider set of
projects, raising both its engagement and its profile.

**Why it matters.** There is a need to move faster on work that delivers benefits at basin scale.
The manual's own phrasing for the rest is blunt: there is strength in numbers, and working together
produces more impact at greater scale.

**Source.** VWBA 2.0 (Version 1, September 2025), Appendix B, consideration 10 — "Opportunity for
collaboration", p. 36. Short restatement at p. 20, where it appears as *Opportunities for
collaboration*, plural.

**Canonical link.** <https://doi.org/10.46830/wrigb.23.00112> — the publisher's record for this
guidebook, World Resources Institute. Not a mirror or a re-hosted copy.

**How to weigh it — guidance, not a gate.**

- Look at opportunities that involve working with others.
- The manual goes one step further than evaluation here and suggests joining a regional collective
  action group, or helping start one, as a way of finding and supporting projects.
- Where several funders are involved, this consideration runs straight into card B-3's division of
  benefit. Collaboration adds value and adds the need to settle attribution.

## Notes for the grader

1. **Page citations were checked against the footers per page, and two considerations straddle a
   break.** Consideration 4 begins on p. 34 and its evaluation guidance finishes on p. 35;
   consideration 9 begins on p. 35 with its reasoning and guidance on p. 36. Both cards say so.
   Every other consideration sits wholly on the page cited. This was established by extracting each
   page separately and seeing which page each heading falls on — not by reading a continuous text
   stream, where a column break and a page break look identical. That distinction is what produced
   the citation error in the Eligibility set, and it is the reason for the method here.

2. **The never-a-bar framing is structural, not decorative.** It appears in the file's opening
   section, in the comparison table, in the group headings, and in the label on every card's fourth
   part — *guidance, not a gate*. A reader arriving at a single card, as Phoebe serves them, still
   meets the framing on that card. That was the point of the per-card requirement, so it seemed
   right to apply it to framing as well as to citations.

3. **Two constraints on Phoebe drawn from the source, not invented.** The manual states the
   considerations carry no priority order, and that companies weigh them differently. Both are
   recorded in the opening section, because both restrict what Phoebe may say: she must not imply
   the numbering is a ranking, and she must not do the weighing on the user's behalf.

4. **Three places where a Feasibility card could be mistaken for an Eligibility one**, each flagged
   on its card:
   - **B-6** looks like criterion 2 but is not. Criterion 2 asks whether a real shared water
     challenge exists; B-6 asks whether the location fits *this company's* stated goals.
   - **B-8** values a project for what it may unlock. That is not a countable benefit, and an
     enabling project still has to satisfy criterion 1 on its own.
   - **B-7** may leave extra benefits described only in words. Phoebe should not let a qualitative
     account read as a quantified one.

5. **Terms of art are kept, sentences are not.** Consideration titles are quoted as labels so the
   cards can be checked against the source, as in the other sets. The manual's example lists —
   financing vehicles in B-9, cost components in B-3 — are name lists rather than prose and are
   carried as names.

6. **One wording discrepancy in the source.** Consideration 10 is titled *Opportunity for
   collaboration* in Appendix B (p. 36) but appears as *Opportunities for collaboration* in the
   Step 2.3 summary box (p. 20). Card B-10 cites the Appendix B form and notes the other. The other
   nine titles match across both places.

7. **The same drop-cap extraction artifact seen in the Eligibility set.** Six of the ten titles come
   out of text extraction with a stray space after the opening letter — *F easible*, *A nticipated*,
   *L ocation*, *O pportunity*, *E nabling*. It is the decorative first letter in the typesetting.
   The titles as written on the cards are correct.

8. **Overlap check run, same method as the committed Eligibility set.** Measured against pp. 34–36
   plus the p. 20 summary box. **At an eight-word window: zero overlapping sequences.** At a
   stricter six-word window: six short runs, being the document's own title in a citation, three
   name lists carried as names (the multiple-benefit categories in B-7, the innovative-finance
   examples in B-9, and *without-project condition* as a defined term), one phrase in B-2, and one
   deliberate short quotation in B-9 — *may be considered with higher priority* — which is marked
   as a quotation because the priority language is the point being made.

   Two passages were rewritten during drafting once the check surfaced them. **One of them was in
   the framing section at the top of this file** — a twelve-word run describing the
   not-a-requirement rule. That it appeared in the most important paragraph in the set, and was
   caught mechanically rather than by eye, is the argument for running the check on every set
   rather than on the ones that feel risky.

9. **Scope.** Appendix B only. This completes the pair Phoebe is named for — Eligibility from
   Appendix A, committed; Feasibility from Appendix B, this file. The activity set from Appendix C
   and the definitions set from the glossary remain drafts awaiting grading.
