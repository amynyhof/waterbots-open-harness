# Project types — the cited list Wellington matches to

**Status: approved by the maintainer, 23 Sep 2026.** Graded at the repository root as a draft
and moved here whole on her word, with one edit from the grade (the "not ruled out" citation).
**This file follows the roster rule, her word of 23 Sep 2026:** drafted here, carried by her hand
to the paid repository, which becomes its source, and never edited here again after that carry; a
build-time check will then hold this site to it, as `check-roster` holds the crew list. The carry
is on item O14's list; until it lands, this copy is the one there is.

**Twenty-five lines.** Twenty water stewardship activity types from the VWBA guidebook's activity
table; four safe-water technology classes from Gold Standard's methodology; and "none of these".
Each line is an id, the standard's own name, one plain sentence a twelve-year-old could read, and
its citation. All wording is a rewrite; no sentence is copied. The source PDFs stay in
`sources-local/`, gitignored, never published.

**What this file is for.** Wellington reads it to match what a visitor says their project does to
one type, or two for a drinking-water project, say the sentence back in his own words, and log the
id only when the visitor says yes (item A16). Phoebe's applies cards read the same ids. The type is
a fact about the activity; which pathway applies is never read from it.

**What it is not.** Not a closed list of what counts. The guidebook says its table covers the
activities most commonly implemented and that an activity absent from it is not thereby ruled out
(Appendix C, pp. 37–39; the note itself on p. 39). That is why line 25 exists. And not a copy of the activity cards: the
untracked Appendix C draft keeps each row's indicators, methods and evidence; this file keeps one
sentence per type and points there.

## Water axis — the guidebook's twenty activity types

Ids follow the untracked activity draft, C-1 to C-20, so the two files point at the same rows.
The category is the table's own.

### Agricultural practices (Table C-1, p. 37)

| Id | Name in the table | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| C-1 | Agricultural best management practices | Farming in ways that help the land hold water and shed less of it, such as cover crops, no-till, terraces, contour planting or managed grazing. |
| C-2 | Agricultural input management | Managing what goes onto fields, such as fertiliser, pesticide or herbicide, so less of it ends up in the water. |
| C-3 | Irrigation efficiency and conversion | Changing how crops are watered so less water is used, such as switching to drip or fixing the system. |

### Demand management (Table C-1, p. 37)

| Id | Name in the table | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| C-4 | Activities that reduce demand | Cutting how much water is taken in the first place, such as buying or leasing water rights so the water stays in the river or aquifer. |
| C-5 | Leak detection and repair | Finding leaks in a water system and fixing them. |
| C-6 | Vegetation and land-use changes that cut water use | Changing what grows on the land so it drinks less, such as removing invasive plants, thinning forest or switching crops. |

### Green or gray infrastructure (Table C-1, pp. 38–39)

| Id | Name in the table | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| C-7 | Runoff capture features | Built features that catch rain and runoff and hold it, such as rain gardens, bioswales or ponds. |
| C-8 | Treatment-focused green infrastructure | Natural-style features built to clean water, such as constructed wetlands. |
| C-9 | Gray infrastructure for treatment | Built plants and pipes that clean wastewater. |
| C-10 | Gray infrastructure for reuse and recycling | Built systems that let used water be cleaned and used again. |
| C-11 | New or alternative water supply | Opening up a new source of water for farms or homes, such as a new well, a rebuilt well, rainwater collection, a piped system or a household connection. |
| C-12 | Instream and habitat restoration | Repairing rivers and streams and the land beside them, such as removing barriers, re-timing dam releases or reconnecting floodplains. |
| C-13 | Recharge-focused infrastructure | Features built to get more water into the ground, such as recharge basins or spreading grounds. |
| C-14 | Wetland and aquatic habitat restoration and creation | Bringing back or making wetlands and other watery habitats that store water. |
| C-15 | Protection of existing water-storing habitat | Agreements such as conservation easements that keep existing wetlands and similar places from being lost. |
| C-16 | Urban surface and runoff management | Managing city surfaces so runoff is cleaner or smaller, such as street sweeping, washing stations or disconnecting paved areas from drains. |

### Land conservation and restoration (Table C-1, p. 39)

| Id | Name in the table | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| C-17 | Preserving existing vegetation cover | Keeping forest, meadow or other land cover as it is instead of clearing it. |
| C-18 | Restoring vegetation cover | Bringing trees, grassland or other cover back onto land that lost it. |

### Water, sanitation and hygiene (Table C-1, p. 39)

| Id | Name in the table | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| C-19 | Access to potable water supply | Giving households or a community access to water that is safe to drink. A project of this type is also asked which of the four carbon classes below it is. |
| C-20 | Access to sanitation | Giving people access to toilets and safe ways to handle waste. |

## Carbon axis — the methodology's four technology classes

A drinking-water project (C-19) takes one of these as well. The class decides which worksheet
rows open on the carbon pathway; a household filter is never asked about boreholes.

| Id | Name in the methodology | In plain words |
|---|---|---|
| HWT | Household water treatment technologies | The water is made safe in the home, at the point where it is drunk, by a filter, chlorine or UV light. (Table 2, p. 6; Table 3, p. 9) |
| IWT | Institutional water treatment technologies | The water is made safe on the premises of a school, clinic, prison or camp, the same ways. (Table 2, pp. 6–7; Table 3, p. 9) |
| CWT | Community level water treatment technologies | The water is made safe at one central point, then piped to homes or collected there at a kiosk or tap. (Table 2, p. 6; Table 3, p. 9) |
| CWS | Community water supply technologies | A new or restored source of water for a community, such as a borehole handpump, a protected spring or a solar pump; if its water is not safe on its own, it is paired with one of the three kinds of treatment above. (Table 2, p. 6; Table 3, p. 9; §3.2.1.1 b, p. 9) |
